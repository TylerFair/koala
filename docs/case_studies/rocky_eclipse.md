# A rocky-planet eclipse with MIRI/LRS

During a secondary eclipse the planet goes behind its star, and the drop in flux is the light the planet itself was giving off.
For a hot rocky planet with MIRI that drop is around 100 ppm, which makes eclipses some of the hardest light curves to fit well.
In this case study we'll fit a synthetic MIRI/LRS eclipse of a made-up planet, ROCKY-1 b, and then look at what happens when the eclipse is too shallow to time.

## Making the data and fitting it

The synthetic observation is generated by a script in the repository, and has a 100 ppm eclipse that's the same at every wavelength, in 14 wavelength channels.
Make it and fit it with:

```bash
python tools/example_phase_curves.py eclipse
koala -c examples/eclipse.yaml
```

The interesting part of the {download}`configuration <../../examples/eclipse.yaml>` is the planet:

```yaml
planet:
  name: ROCKY-1
  period: {value: 2.6162644, prior: fixed}
  eclipse_time: {value: 60001.8081322, prior: uniform, low: 60001.7231322, high: 60001.8931322}
  a_rs: {value: 17.04, prior: gaussian, sigma: 0.5}
  b: {value: 0.208, prior: gaussian, sigma: 0.15, low: 0.0, high: 1.0}
  rprs: {value: 0.0318, prior: fixed}
  eclipse_depth_ppm: {value: 100.0, prior: uniform, low: 0.0, high: 1000.0}
stellar:
  ld_coefficients: [0.0, 0.0]
flags:
  light_curve_model: eclipse
  ld_prior: fixed
```

`light_curve_model: eclipse` switches from a transit to an eclipse, and `eclipse_depth_ppm` is the planet's flux as a fraction of the star's, in ppm.
Instead of the transit time, we give the time of the eclipse, which can be anywhere in a ±2-hour window.
The orbit has Gaussian priors, as they'd come from a transit fit, and the radius ratio is fixed at its transit value.
The star isn't being covered during an eclipse, so its limb darkening doesn't matter, and we fix it at zero.

In this model the planet is a uniformly bright disk.
Outside eclipse the flux is $1 + f_p/f_*$, and during the eclipse it drops to 1 (before the trend is applied).

```{figure} ../_static/eclipse_recovery.png
:alt: The fitted synthetic MIRI eclipse and the recovered emission spectrum

The white-light eclipse and its residuals, and below, the eclipse depth in each channel.
The dashed line is the injected 100 ppm.
```

The white-light eclipse depth comes out at $94.3^{+7.3}_{-7.2}$ ppm, consistent with the 100 ppm we put in, and so does every channel.
The emission spectrum is in `*_Rnative_emission.csv`, with an `eclipse_depth_ppm` column and lower and upper errors.
(The ordinary depth columns in an eclipse run are still $(R_p/R_*)^2$, so be careful not to read those by mistake.)

## When the eclipse is too shallow to time

Now for a harder case: a 70 ppm eclipse on top of a 3,000 ppm exponential ramp, with 350 ppm of noise per integration in white light and 2,000 integrations.

```{figure} ../_static/eclipse_time_scan.png
:alt: A shallow eclipse on an exponential ramp, and a scan of the fit quality against eclipse time

The white-light curve, and a simple least-squares scan over the eclipse time.
The true eclipse is at 60001.8081322 (BJD_TDB − 2400000).
The best depth in the scan is $66.8 \pm 19.9$ ppm, with $\Delta\chi^2 = 11.3$ between the best time and no eclipse.
```

The scan shows the problem: the eclipse is detected, but only weakly, and there are other times that do almost as well.
If we let the eclipse time move over ±2 hours, the posterior includes solutions with almost no eclipse at many different times, and the sampler has a hard time with it.
koala warns you when the white-light eclipse is detected at less than 5σ.
(It also starts every eclipse fit from the best time in a scan like this one, but that can only find a starting point, not add information that isn't in the data.)

The fix is to use what we already know.
If the planet transits, the transit fit gives its orbit and the time of the eclipse much better than a shallow eclipse can, so we fix them:

```yaml
planet:
  name: ROCKY-1
  period: {value: 2.6162644, prior: fixed}
  eclipse_time: {value: 60001.8081322, prior: fixed}
  a_rs: {value: 17.04, prior: fixed}
  b: {value: 0.208, prior: fixed}
  rprs: {value: 0.0318, prior: fixed}
  eclipse_depth_ppm: {value: 70.0, prior: uniform, low: 0.0, high: 1000.0}
```

With the timing fixed, the white-light depth is $68 \pm 20$ ppm, and all 14 channels are consistent with the injected 70 ppm.

A couple of other things to look out for with shallow eclipses.
A ramp with a free timescale can trade off against the eclipse depth, as can a Gaussian process, so check the depth against a simpler trend, and consider leaving out the start of the observation rather than fitting a strong ramp.
And if the model's eclipse doesn't line up with the data at all, check the time system: the eclipse time is in BJD_TDB − 2400000, half a day later than BMJD_TDB.
`eclipse_time` needs a circular orbit and a fixed period, and koala works out the transit time from it as $t_0 = t_{\rm eclipse} - P/2$.
